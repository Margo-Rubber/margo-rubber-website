## 02 The answer up front [The AEO block]

- **Answer block <h2>** (composite):
  · H: “Should you source rubber components from India or China?”
  · Decide it on the things that do not change. Duty positions have moved several times in 2026 and are not a durable basis for a sourcing decision. What is durable is tooling ownership, how your drawing is protected, the real minimum order, how quickly a technical question gets answered, and the total landed cost including freight, duty, inspection and the cost of a bad batch. China is generally stronger on very high volume and on speed at scale. India is generally stronger on smaller runs, on English-language technical dialogue and on tooling terms. Ask both the same questions and compare the answers, not the unit prices.

## 04 Why the duty argument is the wrong one [🛑 No rates, and here is why]

- **H2**: “Why we are not going to quote you a tariff rate”
- **Body**:
  > Most articles with this title are really about duty. Ours is not, for two reasons.
  > The first is that the position moves. Through 2026 the US position on imports from both countries changed several times — agreements reached and measures withdrawn within days of each other, a court ruling, and one measure expiring in July and being replaced immediately by another under a different section of the trade act. Any rate we printed here would be wrong before you finished a tooling programme, and the only rate that matters is the one applying to your HTS code on the day your shipment clears. Get that from your customs broker, or from the current USTR and CBP publications. Not from a supplier’s website.
  > The second reason is more useful. Duty differentials are policy, and policy is the least durable input in a sourcing decision. A supply chain moved for a duty advantage can be stranded by the next revision — and measures introduced in 2026 have applied to groups of economies rather than to a single country, so an advantage assumed on the basis of origin alone may not exist at all.
  > Choose a supplier for the reasons that survive a policy change. Those are what the rest of this article is about.
  _240 words · 🛑 refusing to publish the number, and explaining why, is more persuasive than publishing it — it demonstrates that the publisher understands the volatility better than the pages that quote a figure.
“Applied to groups of economies rather than to a single country” is the important sentence: the naive India-avoids-the-China-tariff argument does not hold as stated, and saying so costs Margo the easiest pitch on the page. That is exactly why it is credible_

## 05 What actually differs [The comparison, on durable ground]

- **H2**: “What actually differs”
- **Table** (Factor | What to look at | Where it usually lands):
  | Minimum order | The figure, the unit, and whether it is per order, per part number, or per part number per compound | India more often accommodates smaller runs; the very lowest unit prices sit at volumes many buyers cannot commit to |
  | Tooling ownership | Who owns the mould, where it is held, and whether it is released on request — in writing, at the order | Negotiable in both, but it must be written down in both. This is the single most common omission we see |
  | Drawing protection | What happens to your drawing, and whether the supplier also sells a competing part | Ask both. A supplier who moulds for you and sells a similar part under its own name is a different risk from one who does not |
  | Technical dialogue | How long a technical question takes to answer, and whether the answer comes from an engineer | India generally has the advantage of English-language engineering conversation and a time zone closer to Europe |
  | Certification | Certificate number, issuing body, expiry and scope — and whether IATF 16949 is held or targeted | Comparable, and it must be checked in both. Ask for the certificate, not the claim |
  | Lead time | From approved sample, not from enquiry; and the shipping leg on top | Depends on the supplier far more than on the country |
  | Compound sourcing | Whether the supplier mixes its own compound or buys it pre-mixed | Both countries have both. India has a domestic natural rubber industry, which matters for natural-rubber parts and not much for synthetics |
  | Scale | Capacity at your volume, and at twice your volume | 🛑 China, clearly, at the top of the volume range |
  _Eight rows, and three of them land on “depends on the supplier” or “check both”. 🛑 That is deliberate. A comparison table where every row favours the publisher’s country is a marketing table, and a procurement manager reads one of those in about four seconds.

## 06 Where Chinese suppliers are stronger [🛑 Written without qualification]

- **H2**: “Where a Chinese supplier is the better choice”
- **Body**:
  > At very high volume, on a commodity part, with a stable drawing and a long horizon, a large Chinese moulder is difficult to beat. The scale is real, the tooling cost per part falls away, and the supply chain around it — materials, secondary processes, packaging, freight consolidation — is dense and fast.
  > Speed to first article at scale is also a genuine advantage, and if your programme needs several tools cut in parallel it is worth taking seriously.
  > If your part is a high-volume commodity seal and price per piece is the deciding factor, we would expect you to source it in China, and we would not try to talk you out of it.
  > Where the picture changes is when the volume is moderate, the part is engineered rather than catalogue, the compound needs tuning to a duty, the drawing is yours and you intend to keep it, or you expect to have technical conversations rather than transactions. That is the work an Indian supplier of our size is built for.
  _193 words · 🛑 the third paragraph is the one that has to survive review. A supplier writing “we would expect you to source it in China and we would not try to talk you out of it” is making a claim about its own judgement rather than its own capability, and it is the most valuable sentence on the page.
The fourth paragraph earns the right to describe the alternative only because the third exists_

## 07 Total landed cost [The frame the live description already names]

- **H2**: “Total landed cost, and the line nobody budgets”
- **Body**:
  > Unit price is the smallest interesting number in a sourcing decision. The comparison that matters adds freight and insurance, duty at whatever rate applies on the day, port and clearance charges, inland transport, inspection, and the working capital tied up in a longer pipeline.
  > Then add the line nobody budgets: what a bad batch costs. Not the value of the parts — the cost of finding out. Containment, sorting, an expedited replacement shipment by air, and a line stopped while you wait. On an engineered part, one bad batch can exceed a year of unit-price saving, which is why the questions in the previous section are commercial questions and not technical ones.
  > Tooling belongs in the calculation too. A mould you own and can move is an asset; a mould you paid for and cannot retrieve is a switching cost that grows every year you stay.
  > Build the comparison as a spreadsheet with those lines in it, and ask both suppliers to fill in the same rows. The answers will differ more than the prices do.
  _211 words · “the cost of finding out” is the phrase that lands, and it reframes quality from an abstraction into a budget line.
The tooling paragraph converts section 05’s row into money, which is the form a procurement manager can act on.
No worked example and no figures — the numbers are the reader’s, and inventing an illustrative case would be the one dishonest move available in this section_
