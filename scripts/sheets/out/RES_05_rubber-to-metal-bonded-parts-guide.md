## 02 The answer up front [The AEO block]

- **Answer block <h2>** (composite):
  · H: “How is rubber bonded to metal?”
  · The bond is made during vulcanisation, not afterwards. The metal is degreased, roughened and coated with a primer and a bonding agent, then placed in the mould. Rubber is injected or compressed onto it and cured, and the bond forms in the press at the same moment the rubber does. Done correctly, the bond is stronger than the rubber — so when a bonded part is pulled apart, the rubber tears and the bond holds.
  _78 words · the whole subject in one paragraph, and the last clause is the sentence the rest of the article defends.
🛑 “The bond forms in the press” is the fact most buyers do not know — they picture glue applied to a finished part. It explains why the metal has to be right before moulding starts, why a bonded part cannot be repaired, and why the insert is a controlled component rather than a fastener_

## 03 The metal decides the bond [Where bonded parts actually fail]

- **H2**: “The metal decides the bond”
- **Body**:
  > Almost every bond failure we are asked to investigate is a surface preparation failure, not a chemistry failure. The bonding agents available today are extremely capable. What they cannot do is stick to oil, to rust, or to a coating that is itself loosely attached.
  > The sequence is degrease, roughen, coat, mould — promptly. Degreasing removes the drawing oils and handling residue; roughening, usually by grit blasting, gives the primer a mechanical key as well as a chemical one; the primer and cover cement go on in a controlled thickness. A prepared insert has a working life. It is not a part you blast on Monday and mould on Friday, and it is not a part anyone should pick up with bare hands — a fingerprint is an oil film in the shape of a finger.
  > 🛑 The most expensive mistake is bonding to a plated or painted insert. The bond will be excellent — to the plating. The joint is then only as strong as the plating’s grip on the steel underneath, which is usually far weaker than the bond and was never designed to carry a structural load. Bond first, then plate the exposed metal if it needs protecting.
  _216 words · 🛑 the plating paragraph is the most valuable in the article. It is a failure a customer causes before the part ever reaches us, it looks like a bonding defect when it lets go, and the argument is completely settled by the sentence “the bond will be excellent — to the plating”.
“A fingerprint is an oil film in the shape of a finger” makes an abstract handling rule concrete in nine words_

## 05 Where bonded parts are used [Including the one Margo already sells and never explains]

## 06 Designing so the bond never fails [🛑 Replaces the “bond area calculation” the description promised]

- **H2**: “Design so the bond is never the weak link”
- **Body**:
  > The design target is not a bond strength figure. It is that the bond is stronger than the rubber — so if the part is ever overloaded, the rubber tears and the interface holds. Size the rubber for the load in the ordinary way, and then make sure nothing in the geometry attacks the bond line.
  > Load the bond in shear or compression, never in peel. A bonded interface is enormously strong across its face and vulnerable at its edge. A load that tries to lift one corner of the rubber off the metal will start a peel, and once a peel starts it runs. Most bonded-part failures that are genuinely design failures are peel failures.
  > Take the rubber over the edge of the metal, or recess the metal. A bond line that ends flush at an exposed edge is both a stress concentration and a path for moisture. Corrosion creeping under the bond edge is the long-term failure mode of an otherwise perfect part, particularly on anything that lives outdoors.
  > Radius the metal. Sharp corners on an insert concentrate stress in the rubber next to them, which is exactly where the bond is.
  > And tolerance the insert. Its position in the mould sets the rubber thickness around it, so the insert’s dimensional variation becomes the part’s variation. An insert drawing with generous tolerances produces a bonded part with none.
  _255 words · 🛑 this section is the honest replacement for “bond area calculation”, and it is more useful than a formula would have been.
“Once a peel starts it runs” is the mechanism in five words.
The insert-tolerance paragraph is the one a customer never expects — they think of the insert as a bought-in part and of the moulding as ours, and the two tolerances stack_

## 07 How a bond is tested [🛑 The failure mode matters more than the force]

- **H2**: “How a bond is tested — and why the number is only half the result”
- **Body**:
  > Bond strength is measured by pulling the bond apart in a defined way. ASTM D429, Rubber Property — Adhesion to Rigid Substrates, is the method set most drawings cite, and it contains several procedures because a bond can be loaded in several ways.
- **Table — ASTM D429** (composite):
    | A | Rubber part assembled between two parallel metal plates |
    | B | 90° stripping test — rubber part assembled to one metal plate |
    | C | Adhesion of rubber to metal measured with a conical specimen |
    | D | Post-vulcanisation bonding of rubber to metal |
    | E | 90° stripping test — rubber tank lining assembled to one metal plate |
    | F | Rubber part assembled between two parallel convex-shaped metal plates |
    | G | Bond durability — double shear cylindrical specimen |
    | H | Bond durability — quadruple shear specimen |
  · The ISO equivalents are ISO 813, a 90° peel test on a rubber strip bonded to a single rigid plate, reported as force divided by specimen width; and ISO 814, the two-plate method, in which a rubber cylinder bonded to two parallel plates is pulled in tension and the breaking force divided by the bonded area gives an adhesion strength in MPa.
- **The failure mode**:
  > 🛑 Here is the part most specifications get wrong. A bond test result is a force and a description of where the specimen broke, recorded with the standard failure-mode symbols the test standards define. The ideal result is that the break is entirely in the rubber — the bond survived and the material gave way, which is exactly what a bonded part should do in service.
  > So a high force with the break at the interface is a worse result than a lower force with the rubber torn. The first says the bond is the weak link and you found its limit. The second says the bond is stronger than the material it is holding, and the number you measured is the rubber’s strength, not the bond’s.
  > A low number can also simply mean a soft compound tore easily. Read the failure mode first, then the force. A specification that asks only for a minimum force has not specified a bond.
  _Article body 305 words plus the table · 🛑 this is the most technically distinctive passage in the whole resources plan. It inverts what a non-specialist assumes — that bigger is better — and it is correct, checkable and almost never explained on a supplier website.
The symbols are referred to but not enumerated, because their definitions live in the standards; naming them without the definitions would be decoration_

## 08 What we test, and what we do not [🛑 The honesty section]

- **H2**: “What we test in-house”
- **Body**:
  > We test tensile strength and elongation to ASTM D412, and compression set to ASTM D395 Method B, in our own lab at Nashik, and those results ship with every consignment alongside a dimensional report and a certificate of conformance.
  > 🛑 Adhesion testing to ASTM D429, ISO 813 or ISO 814 is not on that in-house list. Where a customer’s drawing calls for a bond test, we arrange it through an external laboratory and supply the report. We would rather say that than imply a capability we would subcontract anyway.
  > In production we inspect every bonded part visually and by hand at the bond line, and destructive checks are taken from the batch at an agreed frequency. A bonded part cannot be reworked — there is no second chance at the interface once the part is cured — so the control is in the preparation and the first-off, not in an inspection at the end.
  > We hold ISO 9001:2015, certificate 09111526A, Integral Certification Ltd, valid to 21 February 2027, scope: rubber moulded components — which is what a bonded part is, since the rubber is moulded onto the insert. That is a quality-management certification, not a product approval. We are upgrading to IATF 16949, targeted by the end of 2026.
  _242 words · 🛑 declining the adhesion-test capability on the page whose subject is adhesion is the most credible thing in the article. A reader who has got this far knows the tests exist; claiming them would be the easy move and the one that would not survive a plant visit.
“A bonded part cannot be reworked” explains why the process control sits at the front — and it is the argument for buying from someone who prepares metal properly rather than someone who quotes cheaply_
