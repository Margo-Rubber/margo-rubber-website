## 03 Rubber compound options [🛑 The default swells in the product’s own medium]
> live: LIVE → EPDM (SELECTED), NBR, Neoprene, SBR — on a cap for an oil filler
- **The clearest compound error in the catalogue**:
  > 🛑 EPDM is pre-selected on a part whose first word is “Oil”. It swells badly in mineral oil, and a filler cap sits in hot oil vapour for the life of the engine. A buyer who does not touch the control is quoted a cap that will soften, swell and stop sealing.
  > 🛑 And SBR is on the menu too, with no oil resistance at all. Two of the four compounds offered fail in the medium named in the product title.
  > Margo produces every part in every compound it mixes (client-instructed 2026-09-02). Nothing is removed — all eight are ranked, and the two that fail in oil carry the reason.
- **Selector** (Rank | Compound | Temperature | Why, for an oil filler cap):
  | Recommended | NBR | −30 to +120 °C | The standard oil-service compound and the cheapest sensible choice. Engine oil, gear oil, hydraulic fluid and the vapour above them. This is what should be pre-selected |
  | Recommended | AEM | −40 to +175 °C
continuous to +160 °C | Where the oil runs hotter than NBR can take. The step most suppliers skip, and it costs far less than FKM |
  | Suitable | FKM | −20 to +250 °C
continuous to +200 °C | Hot oil and aggressive fluids, at 2–3× the cost of NBR. Specify it on a measured temperature, not by default |
  | Suitable | Neoprene | −40 to +120 °C | Moderate oil resistance with better weathering — useful where the filler is exposed as well as oily |
  | 🛑 Not recommended | EPDM | −40 to +140 °C | Swells badly in mineral oil. Pre-selected on the live page, and the compound this part must not be made from |
  | 🛑 Not recommended | SBR · natural rubber | See the compound table | No oil resistance at all |
  | Available | Silicone | −60 to +220 °C | Wide temperature range, but it swells in hot mineral oil and tears easily on a part pulled off by hand |
  _Live set: EPDM (SELECTED), NBR, Neoprene, SBR — and two of the four fail in oil, including the one pre-selected.
The closing line does the commercial work: naming NBR as sufficient for most cases is the answer a buyer trusts, and it is also the cheapest thing Margo can quote_

## 06 From compound to certification [The sealing lip and the vent belong in the process]
> live: LIVE H2 → From Compound to Certification · six steps · the same text as all 31 pages
- **Steps** (# | Ships as):
  | 01 | Material compounding — polymer blending, additive integration and batch QC in our in-house lab. The compound is chosen for oil resistance at the filler-neck temperature before anything else. |
  | 02 | Mould design and CNC tool manufacture, in-house, held as a controlled record. The sealing lip and the head grip are cut together, since one sets how well the cap seals and the other whether it can be removed by hand. |
  | 03 | Compression moulding, with the vent formed in the tool where a vented cap is specified. |
  | 04 | Post-cure and deflashing. Flash on the sealing lip is a leak path, and flash across a vent closes it. |
  | 05 | Quality inspection — sealing lip diameter gauged against the neck bore, plus hardness, dimensional check and compression set on batch samples. |
  | 06 | Export packaging — industrial packing with documentation. Caps are bagged loose so the sealing lip is not deformed in transit. ISPM-15 treated pallets where timber packaging is used. |
  _Six steps, six kept. The live text says “ISO-certified lab” in step 01, which becomes “in-house lab”; step 03 says “Compression Moulding” alone and gains the vent; step 04 is “Post-Cure & Trimming” and gains the two flash consequences.
Step 04 is worth the clause because it names two different failures — flash on a lip leaks, flash over a vent blocks it, and a blocked vent on a cap sold as vented is worse than no vent at all, because nobody looks for it.
Step 05 gauges the lip against the bore, which is the check that stops a batch arriving that seals on paper and weeps in service_
