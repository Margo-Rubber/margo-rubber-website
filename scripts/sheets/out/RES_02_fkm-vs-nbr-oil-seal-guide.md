## 02 The answer up front [The AEO block]

- **Answer block <h2>** (composite):
  · H: “FKM or NBR: which should you specify?”
  · Use NBR for mineral oil, fuel, grease and hydraulic fluid up to about +120 °C. It handles most oil duty in industry and costs a fraction of the alternative. Escalate to FKM when the temperature goes beyond nitrile’s range, up to about +250 °C, or when the medium is aggressive — solvents, hot oil, harsh chemicals. Between the two sits AEM, continuous to about +160 °C, which is the right answer more often than either and appears on almost nobody’s menu. And FKM is not universally better: it fails in steam, in brake fluid and in the cold.

## 03 “Viton” is not a specification [🛑 The trademark point, made once and properly]

- **H2**: “A note on “Viton””
- **Body**:
  > FKM (Viton®) is the material class; Viton® is a trademark of The Chemours Company. The two are used interchangeably in everyday specification and they should not be.
  > The trademark covers a family of grades with materially different properties — different fluorine contents, different cure systems, different low-temperature limits. A drawing calling for “Viton 75” has specified a hardness and a brand, and left the chemistry open. Two compounds that both answer that description can behave differently in the same fluid at the same temperature.
  > Specify FKM with the duty, or with an ASTM D2000 line callout. Section 07 shows how. We use FKM throughout the rest of this guide.
  _116 words · 🛑 the trademark is handled once, correctly attributed, and then dropped — which is both the legally clean approach and the technically correct one.
“A hardness and a brand, and left the chemistry open” is the argument in ten words, and it sets up sections 05 and 07_

## 04 NBR is defined by its acrylonitrile content [🛑 The differentiator, half one]

- **H2**: “NBR: the number that is not on your drawing”
- **Body**:
  > Nitrile is a copolymer, and the proportion of acrylonitrile in it is what makes it oil resistant. Commercial grades run from 18 % to 50 % acrylonitrile, and the difference across that range is not marginal.
  > More acrylonitrile means more oil and fuel resistance, and worse flexibility in the cold. The two move in opposite directions, always. There is no grade that is best at both, which is why the question “is nitrile good in oil?” has no single answer.
- **Table** (composite):
    | Low ACN | ~15–25 % | Best low-temperature flexibility and resilience; limited oil resistance | Cold-climate service |
    | Medium ACN | ~25–35 % | Balanced — the general-purpose middle | General hydraulics, around 31–35 % |
    | High ACN | 35 % and above | Maximum oil and fuel resistance; the cold end suffers | Diesel and petrol systems, 36 % and up |
  · So “NBR 70 Shore A” on a drawing is a hardness, not a specification. A seal that hardens on a cold morning and one that swells in diesel can both be supplied against it, entirely correctly. Give the fluid and the cold end, and the acrylonitrile content follows.
  _Article body 168 words plus the table · 🛑 the ACN tradeoff is the single most useful thing a nitrile buyer can be told, and it is almost never on a supplier page — it lives in polymer literature and in the heads of compounders.
The closing line is the practical instruction: the customer does not need to name an ACN percentage, they need to name the two duties that decide it_

  - “acrylonitrile content” and “high nitrile vs low nitrile” are specialist queries with almost no supplier content behind them. This section can own both outright.

## 05 FKM does the same thing with fluorine [🛑 The differentiator, half two]

- **H2**: “FKM: the same trade-off, a different element”
- **Body**:
  > Fluoroelastomer works the same way, and it buys its performance with the same currency. Fluorine content sets the chemical and heat resistance, and the more of it there is, the worse the compound behaves cold.
- **Table** (composite):
    | A | Copolymer of vinylidene fluoride and hexafluoropropylene | ~66 % | The general-purpose fluoroelastomer |
    | F | Terpolymer adding tetrafluoroethylene | 68–69.5 % | Better chemical and heat resistance, at the cost of compression set and low-temperature flexibility |
    | GLT | Low-temperature grade | ~65 % | Flexible to roughly −40 °C; not for flex fuels or aggressive solvents |
    | GFLT | Low-temperature grade retaining chemical resistance | ~67–68 % | The compromise grade — good cold performance with fuel and chemical resistance retained |
  · This is why the low-temperature limit is the number to check on any FKM specification. A standard grade is commonly limited to around −20 °C. A compound chosen entirely for heat can fail on a cold start, and the customer will describe it as a seal that leaked in winter rather than as a specification error.
  · Low-temperature grades exist and reach roughly −40 °C. They cost more and, in the case of GLT, give up some chemical resistance to get there. Tell us the cold end and we will pick the grade; do not assume the standard one covers it.
  _Article body 156 words plus the table · 🛑 the structural parallel with section 04 is the article’s architecture — two families, one trade-off, the same lesson: the grade matters as much as the polymer.
“A seal that leaked in winter rather than a specification error” names how this failure is actually reported, which is why it recurs_

  - No trademark in this table. Type letters and fluorine contents are the material facts; brand names are not.

## 06 Where FKM is the wrong answer [The list nobody selling FKM publishes]

- **H2**: “Where FKM is the wrong answer”
- **Body**:
  > Fluoroelastomer is expensive, so it is treated as the safe choice. It is not universally better than nitrile, and there are duties where it is clearly worse.
  > Steam and hot water. FKM is a poor choice in steam; EPDM is the right answer there, and specifying a fluoroelastomer because it is the premium option is how that mistake happens.
  > Glycol-based brake fluid. DOT 3 and DOT 4 attack FKM. EPDM again.
  > Ketones, esters and ethers. Acetone and MEK are the familiar examples. Worth noting that biodiesel is a fatty-acid methyl ester — an ester — which catches people out on fuel-system parts.
  > Amines and ammonia. Common in refrigeration and in some process chemistry.
  > And the cold, as section 05 sets out. Where FKM genuinely runs out — on chemistry rather than temperature — the escalation is FFKM, at a further large step in cost.
  _175 words · 🛑 a page selling FKM listing five duties where FKM is wrong is the most trust-building block in the article, and every item is a real, checkable incompatibility.
The biodiesel line is the one readers will remember — it is a modern fuel, it is an ester, and nobody thinks of it that way_

## 07 The side-by-side, and how to specify [Cost-in-use, kept from the live description]

- **H2**: “FKM and NBR, side by side”
- **Table** (composite):
    | Temperature range | −30 to +120 °C | −20 to +250 °C
continuous to +200 °C | FKM hot, NBR cold |
    | Mineral oil, fuel, grease | Very good — set by ACN content | Excellent, including hot oil | NBR is enough for most duty |
    | Solvents and aggressive chemicals | Limited | Excellent | FKM |
    | Steam and hot water | Poor | Poor | Neither — use EPDM |
    | Glycol brake fluid | Poor | Poor | Neither — use EPDM |
    | Ozone, UV, weathering | Poor | Very good | FKM |
    | Hardness range | 40–90 Shore A | 60–90 Shore A | NBR covers more, softer |
    | Relative cost | Low | High | NBR, by a wide margin |
  · Cost-in-use, not unit price. A fluoroelastomer seal that outlasts three nitrile ones in a hot application is cheaper, once the downtime of two changeovers is counted. A fluoroelastomer seal specified for a duty nitrile would have handled is simply more expensive. The question is not which material is better — it is whether the duty justifies the escalation.
- **Specifying it**:
  > ASTM D2000 does this compactly, and it is the format to put on the drawing. Its Type letter is the heat-ageing temperature and its Class letter is the maximum oil swell, so nitriles typically fall in the BF, BG and BK classes as oil resistance increases, and fluoroelastomers in HK — Type H for 250 °C ageing, Class K for the tightest swell requirement. The full Type and Class tables are in our gasket material selection guide.
  > Or simply tell us the fluid, both temperatures and the hardness, and we will write the callout with you.
  _Article body 173 words plus the table · “cost-in-use” was already in the live description and it is the right frame — the article earns it by defining it in two sentences rather than using it as a slogan.
The D2000 paragraph makes the letter pairs mean something: BF to BK is the ACN story from section 04, expressed in the standard’s own notation_
