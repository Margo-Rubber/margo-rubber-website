## 03 Questions 1–2 — the certificate and the compound [The two nobody asks properly]

- **Q1 <h3>** (composite):
  · H: “1. What is your certificate number, who issued it, when does it expire — and what is its scope?”
  · “We are ISO 9001 certified” is not an answer. A certificate has a number, a named certification body, an expiry date and — the part almost nobody asks for — a scope.
  · The scope says what the certificate covers. A quality system scoped to rubber moulded components does not cover die-cut sheet parts or fabricated assemblies, even if the same company makes them. A supplier who quotes the scope line without being pushed is telling you they have read their own certificate.
  · And ISO 9001 is a quality-management certification, not a product approval. It says the company controls its processes. It says nothing about whether a part is fit for drinking water, food contact or a medical device — those are separate approvals, held separately.
- **Q2 <h3>** (composite):
  · H: “2. Do you mix your own compound, or buy it in?”
  · This one question separates a moulder from a manufacturer. A company that mixes its own compound can adjust hardness, cure system and filler loading to your duty. A company buying pre-mixed stock can offer you what its supplier sells.
  · It also decides traceability. In-house mixing means the batch record starts with the raw polymer; bought-in compound means it starts at goods inward, and a compound problem is someone else’s to investigate.
  _Article body 232 words · 🛑 the scope paragraph in Q1 is the most useful single idea in the article, and it is one this content set applied to Margo’s own certificate on five separate SKU sheets.
Q2 is the question that most changes a shortlist and it is almost never asked, because buyers assume every rubber factory mixes_

## 04 Questions 3–4 — testing and minimums [Where the short answer beats the long one]

- **Q3 <h3>** (composite):
  · H: “3. Which tests do you run in-house, and which do you subcontract?”
  · Note the shape of the question. Almost every supplier can produce a long list of standards. Far fewer will tell you which of them happen in their own building.
  · Neither answer is wrong — subcontracting a specialist test to an accredited laboratory is normal and often better than doing it badly in-house. What matters is that the supplier knows and says which is which. A supplier who claims everything is in-house, on a list including tests that need equipment few moulders own, is either mistaken or guessing.
- **Q4 <h3>** (composite):
  · H: “4. What is the minimum order — and per what?”
  · “Our minimum is 500” means nothing without a unit and a scope. Five hundred what — pieces, kilograms, rupees? And per what — per order, per part number, or per part number per compound?
  · The last one catches people out. The same part in two compounds is two part numbers and two minimums, because they are two different production runs with two different mixes. A supplier who has not made that clear at quotation has not quoted your job.
  · Ask about prototypes separately. A high production minimum is normal for a moulder; a supplier who cannot make you a first article without it is a different problem.
  _Article body 218 words · Q3 is engineered so that honesty scores highest, which is the only way this article works — it has to reward the answer Margo actually gives.
🛑 Q4 is the question Margo loses on volume and wins on clarity. A 10,000-piece minimum is high; stating it precisely, per part number per compound, with prototypes carved out, is what a buyer can plan around. Section 08 says so plainly rather than hiding it_

## 05 Questions 5–6 — tooling and evidence [🛑 Question 6 is the one this website fails]

- **Q5 <h3>** (composite):
  · H: “5. Who owns the tooling, and where is it kept?”
  · Nobody asks this until they want to leave. If you paid for the mould, say so in writing at the order, and establish that it is released to you or to another supplier on request.
  · A mould that a supplier holds and will not release is a commercial lock, whatever the commercial relationship looks like while it is going well. Ask also whether the tool is maintained, how many shots it has run, and what happens when it wears out — a mould is a consumable with a long life, not a permanent asset.
- **Q6 <h3>** (composite):
  · H: “6. Can you show me a photograph of a part like mine?”
  · Not a stock image of a factory. Not a rendering. A photograph of a part they made.
  · It is the simplest question on this list and the most revealing, because a manufacturer photographing its own work has work to photograph. A catalogue illustrated entirely with library images of machinery is telling you something, and often what it is telling you is that the catalogue was assembled rather than made.
  · Follow it with: can I see the part in production, or visit? A supplier who welcomes a visit and a supplier who deflects one are different suppliers.

Publishing Q6 is a commitment to fix that — and it is a good commitment to make, because the photography gap is the largest single defect this whole content set has found. Do not ship the article before at least one real product photograph is live._

## 06 Questions 7–8 — capacity and what happens when it goes wrong [The two that matter after the first order]

- **Q7 <h3>** (composite):
  · H: “7. What happens if my volume doubles?”
  · A trial order tells you nothing about capacity. Ask what the current lead time is, what it becomes at twice the quantity, and whether that needs a second tool or a second shift.
  · Ask how many active customers they serve. A supplier with a handful of customers has capacity and concentration risk; one with hundreds has neither capacity to spare nor much interest in a small account. Both are workable. Not knowing which you are dealing with is not.
- **Q8 <h3>** (composite):
  · H: “8. What ships with the consignment — and what happens when a batch is wrong?”
  · Every consignment should arrive with a material test report, a dimensional inspection report and a certificate of conformance, and for export, a commercial invoice, packing list and certificate of origin. If any of that is negotiable, ask why.
  · Then ask the harder question. A batch will be wrong eventually. What is contained, how fast, who investigates, and what do you receive in writing? A supplier with a real answer has been through it. A supplier who says it has never happened has either not been making parts for very long or is not counting.
  _Article body 205 words · Q8’s last sentence is the sharpest line in the article, and it is the sort of thing only a manufacturer can say without sounding cynical.
Q7 deliberately declines to say that more customers is better — both shapes are workable, and the failure is not knowing which you have bought_
