## 02 The answer up front [The AEO block]

- **Answer block <h2>** (composite):
  · H: “Silicone or EPDM: which should you use?”
  · Use EPDM for steam, hot water and anything outdoors — it is far tougher, far cheaper, and peroxide-cured grades hold steam to around +135 °C. Use silicone for dry heat above about +150 °C, where EPDM has run out, and for cold below −40 °C. Use neither in mineral oil, fuel or grease — both swell, and the answer there is nitrile or a fluoroelastomer. Where both would work, EPDM wins on tear strength and on price.
  _84 words · four sentences, four decisions, no hedging. The last one is the one buyers need: silicone reads as the premium material and gets specified by default, and on most duties where both are viable it is the weaker and more expensive choice_

## 03 The disqualifier [Ask the question that ends the comparison]

- **H2**: “First, is there oil?”
- **Body**:
  > If the seal touches mineral oil, fuel or grease, this comparison is over before it starts. Both compounds swell in petroleum media — EPDM badly, silicone significantly — and a swollen seal grows, softens and extrudes out of its groove.
  > The answer there is nitrile up to about +120 °C, AEM to about +160 °C continuous, or a fluoroelastomer above that. It is worth saying plainly, because silicone’s temperature range makes it look like the obvious choice for a hot engine-bay part — and a hot engine-bay part is usually a hot oily part.
  > Ask what the seal touches before you ask how hot it gets. Temperature narrows the field; the fluid decides it.
  _129 words · 🛑 opening a comparison article by disqualifying both candidates is unusual and it is the right call — the commonest way this decision goes wrong is that neither material was appropriate.
The engine-bay sentence names the specific trap: silicone’s −60 to +220 °C reads as the safe pick for anything hot, and oil is what actually kills it_

## 04 “EPDM to 140 °C” hides a decision [🛑 The article’s differentiator]

- **H2**: “EPDM is two materials, and the cure system decides which one you get”
- **Body**:
  > Almost every datasheet gives EPDM a single temperature range. The range depends on how the compound was crosslinked, and the two options are not close.
  > Sulphur-cured EPDM is the standard, economical grade, and it is good to around +120 °C. Peroxide-cured EPDM reaches around +150 °C — and in steam the gap is wider than the headline numbers suggest: peroxide-cured EPDM resists steam to around +135 °C, while sulphur-cured EPDM is far lower.
  > The reason is the crosslink itself. Peroxide curing forms carbon–carbon bonds, which carry more energy than the sulphur bridges of a conventional cure — so a peroxide-cured compound holds up better to heat and oxidation, and it also gives a lower compression set, which is what a seal actually needs over years of service.
  > So “EPDM” on a drawing is an incomplete specification for anything hot. Say which cure system, or say the temperature and the medium and let the manufacturer choose.
  _191 words · 🛑 this is the section that makes the guide worth publishing. It is checkable, it is rarely explained on a supplier site, and it is the reason a customer’s EPDM seal failed at a temperature their datasheet said was fine.
It also quietly explains Margo’s own published −40 to +140 °C — a single figure sitting between the two cure systems — without contradicting it_

## 05 Steam is not the same as heat [The dividing line]

- **H2**: “Steam is not the same as heat”
- **Body**:
  > A compound’s dry-heat rating does not tell you how it behaves in steam. Steam is hot water under pressure, and it attacks elastomers chemically as well as thermally — so a material can hold up to +200 °C in dry air and be a poor choice at +130 °C in a steam line.
  > EPDM is the steam compound. Peroxide-cured grades handle steam to around +135 °C, and EPDM is the standard answer for hot water and steam service across the process industries. Silicone is rated far higher in dry heat, and it is not the first choice in pressurised steam, though certain grades are used for steam sterilising duty at around +150 °C.
  > So the dividing line is not a temperature. It is whether the heat arrives wet or dry. Wet and hot: EPDM. Dry and very hot: silicone. And if it is wet, hot and oily, it is neither.
  _173 words · “the dividing line is not a temperature” is the sentence the whole article turns on, and it is the correction to how the comparison is usually framed — as two numbers on a chart.
The three-way closing line ties section 03 back in in eleven words_

## 07 What silicone is bad at [The counterweight to its reputation]

- **H2**: “What silicone is not good at”
- **Body**:
  > Silicone has a reputation as the premium elastomer, and on temperature it earns it. Everywhere else it is a compromise, and the compromises are worth knowing before it is specified by default.
  > It tears. Silicone’s tear strength is low compared with almost every other sealing compound, which matters on thin sections, on parts that are stretched over something during assembly, and on anything with a sharp corner in the tooling. A silicone part that survives its duty perfectly can be destroyed by a fitter’s thumb.
  > It abrades. That rules it out of most dynamic duty — reciprocating seals, anything sliding in a bore — where EPDM or nitrile would last far longer.
  > And it costs. Silicone is materially more expensive than EPDM, and on a part where both would work the cost difference is real at volume.
  > Specify silicone when the temperature or the cold demands it. On any other basis, EPDM is usually the better engineering answer as well as the cheaper one.
  _183 words · 🛑 an article arguing against the more expensive material it also sells is the pattern that makes a technical page trusted, and Margo mixes both, so there is no commercial cost to saying it.
“Destroyed by a fitter’s thumb” is the memorable line, and it describes a real and common failure that no datasheet predicts_

## 08 Specifying it properly [D2000, cure system, post-cure]

- **H2**: “How to specify either one so it arrives right”
- **Body**:
  > Name the material, the temperature, the medium and the hardness — and for EPDM, the cure system. A drawing that says only “EPDM 70 Shore A” leaves the most important decision to whoever quotes it cheapest.
  > ASTM D2000 does this compactly, because its Type letter is the heat-ageing temperature and its Class letter is the oil-swell requirement. EPDM compounds typically fall in the AA and CA classes and silicones in the FE and GE classes — and both sit in Class A, no oil-swell requirement, which is the standard’s way of saying what section 03 says in a sentence.
  > For silicone, ask about post-cure. A post-cure drives off residual volatiles and is what lets a silicone part reach its published compression-set figures. A silicone seal that has not been post-cured will not behave like the datasheet, and on a long-life static seal that is the difference between working and slowly leaking.
  _160 words · the D2000 paragraph links this guide to the gasket material guide and makes both stronger — the same specification language applied to a different decision.
🛑 “Both sit in Class A, no oil-swell requirement” is a neat proof of the disqualifier: the standard itself declines to ask either compound to resist oil_
